## Compressing the dataset

### Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

- Reducing the dimensionality of a dataset by measuring the key components driving difference between observations.
- Here, turning lots of variables (e.g. education is actually comprised of "Share with no qualifications", "Share with degree-level education", etc.) into one combination of all of these variables which well-captures differences between LSOAs. <!-- .element: class="fragment" data-fragment-index="1" -->

</section>

<section>

We can take a complex dataset which is defined by multiple variables (here 3, on the left), and perform PCA to produce vectors (the axes on the middle graph) which capture the variance between observations. 

<img src="{{asset_folder}}/pca_example.png">

PC1 captures *the most* variance, then PC2, etc.

</section>

---

So in this case, the first principal component (PC1) might look something like: 

<div>
  \[ \begin{aligned} &amp; 0.4 \times \text{pub_density} + 0.3 \times \text{proportion_professional_occupation} + \\ 
  &amp; 0.2 \times \text{cafe_price} + 0.1 \times \text{house_prices} \end{aligned} \]
</div>

We can then use PC1 to produce one score per LSOA. <!-- .element: class="fragment" data-fragment-index="1" -->

---

**Splitting up the dataset (K-means clustering)** <!-- .element: class="r-fit-text" -->

Used to split a dataset into K groups, here two groups of LSOAs based on their score from the PCA.

<img src="{{asset_folder}}/k_means.png">

The two groups of LSOAs can then be classified, with a line defining where London moves between group 1 and group 2. <!-- .element: class="fragment" data-fragment-index="1" -->

There was then a validation step, to see how often a model can correctly predict which side of the "line" an LSOA should be on. The geography of the LSOAs wasn't taken into account at any point. <!-- .element: class="fragment" data-fragment-index="2" -->
